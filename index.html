Should that money go in a 529?

A 529 shelters investment gains from tax. That is the entire benefit. So the answer turns on two things most people never check before funding one: how much gain there will actually be by the time the tuition bill lands, and how sure you are the money gets spent on school. The 529 below follows a real age-based glide path, so it de-risks as enrollment approaches. The taxable account does not, which is exactly where most comparisons quietly go wrong.

What the math is doing

Every assumption, and where each one can mislead you.

The benefit is tax-free growth. Nothing else.

Contributions are not federally deductible. Not at any income, not in any state. The money going in is already taxed.

So the 529's entire value is that gains escape tax on the way out. No gain, no benefit. A shelter over an account that did not grow is a shelter over nothing, and you paid a fee premium for it.

The line worth remembering: tax optimization is not the same as wealth optimization. Paying zero tax on a small gain can leave you poorer than paying tax on a large one.
Why the glide path matters, and what it proves

Age-based 529 portfolios do not hold one allocation. They start near 95% stocks and step down to about 12% by enrollment, so the money rides equity risk in the early years and is protected by the time tuition is due. The schedule here is Vanguard’s published Target Enrollment allocation as of 1 July 2026.

Over 18 years that glide blends to roughly 6.8% a year at 9% stocks and 4% bonds. Not the 9% the stock knob says, and not the 4% the bond knob says. The average is dominated by the early years, because that is when the balance compounds longest.

Here is the part worth sitting with. Click Match my 529’s blended rate so both accounts earn the same thing, and the 529 wins at every horizon. It never flips. Same return, same risk, one of them tax-free — of course it wins.

Now drag the taxable rate back up. It takes surprisingly little — a point or two — before the taxable account wins again. But every one of those points is risk you are choosing to carry right up to the tuition bill, at the exact moment the 529 is deliberately stepping out of the market.

So if your real 529 lost to your real brokerage account, the tax code almost certainly was not the reason. The brokerage account was taking more risk.

That is the trap in the whole 529 debate. People compare a de-risked college fund against an aggressive portfolio, see the gap, and conclude something about tax law. They have measured their own asset allocation.

The certainty knob is the one people skip

On a non-qualified withdrawal, income tax and the 10% additional tax hit only the earnings, never the principal. So the penalty scales with how well the account did.

This produces the counterintuitive result worth sitting with: a 529 that performed badly is cheap to walk away from, and one that performed well is expensive. Success builds the cage. Drag the certainty knob below 100% and watch which direction the advantage moves.

Qualified means tuition, mandatory fees, books, supplies, equipment, computers, and room and board within the school's stated allowance. It does not mean transportation, even when the school includes travel in its official cost of attendance for financial aid. Same student, same school, two different definitions.

How much can you actually put in?

There is no federal contribution limit on a 529. Not $150,000, not any number. What exists are three separate things people conflate:

1. The annual gift exclusion — $19,000 per donor, per beneficiary in 2026 ($38,000 for a married couple splitting gifts). Stay under it and there is nothing to file.

2. Superfunding, the five-year election. 529s uniquely let you drop five years of exclusions in at once and elect to treat it as five equal annual gifts: $95,000 single, $190,000 married in 2026. It requires filing Form 709 for that tax year. Miss the form and you have an unreported gift.

3. State aggregate maximums — roughly $235,000 to $675,000 per beneficiary, across all accounts in that state's plan. Once the balance hits the cap, no new contributions. This is the only real ceiling, and it is set by the state, not the IRS.

Where the $150,000 idea comes from: five years of gifts for a married couple at the 2021 exclusion of $15,000 came to exactly $150,000. It was never a contribution cap — it was that year's front-loading limit, and it has moved every few years since.
What this model leaves out

The Roth rollover. Since 2024 you can move unused 529 money into the beneficiary's Roth IRA: $35,000 lifetime, account must be at least 15 years old, contributions from the last 5 years excluded, capped annually at the Roth limit, and the beneficiary needs earned income. This is the real answer to "what if my kid doesn't go" and it makes the certainty knob much less scary if you started early enough to clear 15 years.

Changing the beneficiary. You can move the account to a qualifying family member. Worth real money if a sibling is years behind. Worth little if they are two years behind, which only delays the same problem.

Financial aid. A parent-owned 529 counts as a parental asset and is assessed lightly. Not modeled here.

Losses are not deductible. Claiming a loss on a 529 was a miscellaneous itemized deduction subject to the 2% floor, and those have been suspended since 2018.

The honest verdict

Run it yourself. Fund it at birth, certainty at 100%, and click Match my 529’s blended rate so neither account is secretly taking more risk. The 529 wins comfortably.

Now drag the funding age to 17. The 529 still wins, but by pocket change, because there was never time to accumulate the gains the shelter exists to protect. You bought a tax shelter, and paid a plan fee for it, to protect almost nothing.

Drag it the other way, to a grandparent funding before the child is born, and the advantage compounds for more than two decades. Same instrument, same tax code. Only the start date moved.

Which means the failure mode is not the instrument. It is funding it late and expecting a deduction that was never there. Fund early and a 529 is very good. Fund at seventeen and you have bought a tax shelter for a gain that will not arrive.
